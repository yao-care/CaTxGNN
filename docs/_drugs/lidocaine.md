# Lidocaine: From Local Anesthesia to Conjunctival Disorder (SUNCT/SUNA Spectrum)

## One-Sentence Summary

Lidocaine is a well-established amide-type local anesthetic and Class Ib antiarrhythmic; detailed original-indication and MOA records are not available in this evidence pack. Among 10 TxGNN-predicted indications, the single highest-scoring prediction (**punctate epithelial keratoconjunctivitis**, 99.99%) has zero supporting trials or literature and is mechanistically contradicted by lidocaine's known corneal toxicity — it is flagged in the source data itself as a candidate to exclude rather than pursue. The best-evidenced candidate is instead **Conjunctival Disorder**, anchored in lidocaine's established off-label IV use for SUNCT/SUNA headache and its standard role as an ophthalmic local anesthetic, with **18 clinical trials** and **20 publications** identified, though much of that evidence reflects existing anesthetic use rather than a genuinely new indication.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in this evidence pack. Based on known pharmacology, lidocaine is a voltage-gated sodium-channel blocker used for local/regional anesthesia and as a Class Ib antiarrhythmic; its efficacy in these established uses is well documented, and mechanistically it may extend to conditions involving neurally mediated ocular surface symptoms.

The concrete signal behind the "Conjunctival Disorder" prediction is narrower than the label suggests: it is driven almost entirely by evidence for **SUNCT/SUNA** (Short-lasting Unilateral Neuralgiform headache attacks with Conjunctival injection and Tearing/cranial Autonomic symptoms) — a trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia in which conjunctival injection and tearing are defining features. Intravenous lidocaine is already used off-label as an acute-phase treatment for refractory SUNCT/SUNA cases, supported by an open-label prospective study with single-arm meta-analysis and multiple clinical reviews. Separately, a large body of ophthalmic-surgery trials confirms lidocaine's standard role as a subconjunctival/peribulbar/topical anesthetic (e.g., pterygium excision, intravitreal injection, cataract surgery) — this reflects lidocaine's *existing* approved-class use, not a new indication, but does support route/tissue compatibility with the conjunctiva.

In short, the actionable hypothesis is narrower and more specific than "conjunctival disorder" as a whole: it is lidocaine (IV) for the SUNCT/SUNA subgroup, where conjunctival injection is a clinical sign rather than the primary disease target.
