# Arsenic Trioxide: From Acute Promyelocytic Leukemia to Myelodysplastic Syndrome

## One-Sentence Summary

Arsenic trioxide is an injectable anticancer drug originally used for acute promyelocytic leukemia and other acute myeloid leukemias.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)**. The data compiled for this report link this prediction to **19 clinical trials** and **20 publications**, including one 2023 systematic review and network meta-analysis. Most of the trials are small, early-phase, or terminated.

The report evaluates the strongest-supported prediction (rank 6, myelodysplastic syndrome) rather than rank 1, "unclassified myelodysplastic syndrome". Rank 1 has the highest TxGNN score but no linked trials or literature (Evidence Level L5, Hold).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data for arsenic trioxide (ATO) is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on the linked studies, ATO induces apoptosis (through NF-κB/FLIP and BCL2-family modulation) and promotes cell differentiation. A newer Phase 1 trial proposes that it also reactivates TP53.

APL is a clonal bone marrow malignancy in which ATO has well-established efficacy. MDS is also a clonal disorder of blood-forming stem cells, with ineffective hematopoiesis and a risk of progression to acute myeloid leukemia. The shared biology of abnormal, apoptosis-resistant myeloid cells makes the extrapolation plausible. The literature notes that ATO is effective in about 20% of MDS patients, which is modest but real.

Several other top predictions are less well supported. Aregenerative anemia (rank 4) has small clinical reports in refractory aplastic anemia. Ewing sarcoma (rank 7) has mainly preclinical support. The remaining predictions have no linked evidence.

The sampled entries show **Major** interactions with QT-prolonging or electrolyte-disturbing agents, including cisapride, clarithromycin, dolasetron, granisetron, levofloxacin, hydrocortisone, and several amphotericin B formulations. **Moderate** interactions include famotidine, loperamide, and laxatives such as bisacodyl, lactulose, and magnesium products. The Health Canada package insert warnings and contraindications were not captured in the data compiled for this report. Please refer to the package insert for full safety information.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
MDS is the best-supported prediction for ATO, with multiple completed Phase 1/2 trials, a 2023 systematic review, and mechanistic data. However, the trials are small or terminated, no MDS-specific Phase 3 trial exists, and response rates are modest (about 17-20%). The knowledge-graph contraindications (QT prolongation, torsades de pointes, electrolyte disturbance) call for strict cardiac safeguards. The other nine predictions have limited or no evidence and should remain on Hold or be treated as research questions.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Health Canada package insert warnings and contraindications. This is a blocking gap for safety screening.
- Mechanism-of-action data from DrugBank.
- A critical appraisal of the 2023 network meta-analysis and results from the ongoing oral ATO trials (NCT06670222, NCT06778187).
- A cardiac and electrolyte monitoring plan covering ECG/QTc, potassium and magnesium, and a review of concomitant QT-prolonging medications.
- Confirmation of approved indications on the four Canadian DINs, since the licence records contain no indication text.
