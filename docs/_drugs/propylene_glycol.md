# Propylene Glycol: From Dry Eye Syndrome to Bronchitis

## One-Sentence Summary

Propylene glycol is a solvent and lubricant used in ocular lubricant products, originally associated with dry eye syndrome and xerophthalmia.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **bronchitis**, but the evidence does not support this: there are **0 clinical trials** and **3 publications**, and those publications mostly point to possible airway harm rather than benefit.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, propylene glycol is used as an ocular lubricant and as a solvent or excipient in many formulations. Its use in dry eye is as a moisturising and lubricating agent, not as a disease-modifying drug.

The data compiled for this report do not support a therapeutic link between dry eye and bronchitis. The retrieved literature discusses propylene glycol as a constituent of e-cigarette liquids and its potential to irritate the airways or worsen lung disease, including asthma and chronic bronchitis. This suggests possible harm rather than benefit. The third paper (quercetin in mice) does not involve propylene glycol as a therapeutic agent.

The high TxGNN score is therefore best read as a network-based prediction without clinical backing.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction rests on a model score alone (Evidence Level L5). No clinical trials exist for bronchitis, and the available literature raises a possible airway-irritation concern. No therapeutic mechanism is supported.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data for propylene glycol
- Health Canada package insert warnings and contraindications, so that safety screening can begin
- Direct clinical or mechanistic evidence for any respiratory benefit, plus a review of inhaled-exposure safety
- Review of other predicted indications. Diabetic retinopathy (L4) has only indirect evidence: one trial that used artificial tears as a comparator for injection pain, and a paper on a different compound, propylene glycol mannate sulfate. The other predictions, mainly cataract types, are model-only with no supporting evidence.
