# Carbamazepine: From Epilepsy and Trigeminal Neuralgia to Trigeminal Nerve Neoplasm

## One-Sentence Summary

Carbamazepine is an established antiseizure medication, originally used for epilepsy (including partial and generalized tonic-clonic seizures), bipolar disorder, and trigeminal and glossopharyngeal neuralgia.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **trigeminal nerve neoplasm**, but there are **no registered clinical trials** and the **20 publications** retrieved are mostly case reports and reviews of tumor-associated trigeminal neuralgia.
They support pain control only, not any effect on the tumor itself.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available from the data compiled for this report. Based on known information, carbamazepine is a sodium channel–blocking antiseizure drug. It is well established for trigeminal neuralgia, where it reduces ectopic neuronal firing. Its efficacy in that condition suggests a plausible, symptom-level link to the predicted indication.

The relationship between the original and predicted indications is largely a matter of proximity. Trigeminal neuralgia can be caused by vascular compression or by a tumor involving the trigeminal nerve. The retrieved literature describes tumor-associated or secondary trigeminal neuralgia (for example venous angioma, lipoma, dermoid cyst, meningioma, schwannoma and lymphoma). In these reports carbamazepine was used for pain, and several patients responded poorly or stopped responding as the underlying lesion progressed.

**Important caveat:** the evidence supports symptomatic pain control, not antineoplastic activity. The very high TxGNN score most likely reflects the closeness of this disease node to the already-approved trigeminal neuralgia indication. No evidence suggests that carbamazepine treats the tumor itself.

## Safety Considerations

**Contraindications flagged in the knowledge graph** (drug-disease associations, not label text):
- Blood and bone marrow disorders: thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, agranulocytosis, pancytopenia, anemia, and congenital or cyclic neutropenia syndromes
- Cardiac: heart conduction disease, atrioventricular block, coronary artery disease, sudden cardiac arrest, hypertensive disorders
- Liver, kidney and gallbladder disease, including impaired renal function
- Glaucoma and ocular hypertension
- Inappropriate ADH syndrome and nephrogenic syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis
- Absence epilepsy, anxiety and neurotic disorders, dysthymic disorder

**Drug Interactions:** 100 interactions were found. Of the 20 reviewed, two are classed as Major: **clarithromycin** and **eliglustat**. Most of the others are Moderate, including doxycycline, corticosteroids (hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, betamethasone, triamcinolone), vitamin D analogues (cholecalciferol, calcifediol, calcitriol), pioglitazone, bupropion, aprepitant, morphine, metronidazole, canagliflozin, cimetidine, dronabinol and activated charcoal.

Package insert warnings were not available, so please refer to the Health Canada package insert for the full safety information.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The evidence is at L4: there are no clinical trials, and the literature consists of case reports and reviews describing carbamazepine as symptomatic therapy for tumor-related facial pain. There is no sign of antineoplastic activity. The high model score probably reflects proximity to the existing trigeminal neuralgia indication rather than a true new therapeutic effect.

**Other predictions in the data (all with L4–L5 evidence, no trials):**
- *Reflex and startle epilepsies* (startle epilepsy, audiogenic, reading, eating, thinking, micturition-induced and orgasm-induced seizures): mechanistically plausible extensions of the approved focal epilepsy indication. Support is indirect, mostly case reports and animal models, and nothing is specific to carbamazepine in most of them. Startle epilepsy, audiogenic seizures and reading seizures are better framed as research questions.
- *Beta-ketothiolase deficiency and congenital Rett syndrome variant*: model prediction only, with no literature and no plausible mechanism beyond symptomatic seizure control.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data and Health Canada package insert warnings and contraindications (currently missing, and the missing label data blocks safety screening)
- Approved indication text and dosage form data for the Canadian licences
- Clarification of whether the goal is symptomatic pain control in tumor-associated trigeminal neuralgia, which is already covered by the existing indication, or a genuine antitumor effect, for which there is no support
- For any reflex-epilepsy direction, controlled or well-documented clinical data specific to carbamazepine
- A safety plan covering haematological monitoring and HLA-related skin reaction risk, and attention to the major drug interactions
