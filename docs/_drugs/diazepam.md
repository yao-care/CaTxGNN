## One-Sentence Summary

Diazepam is a benzodiazepine that enhances GABA-A receptor activity and is marketed in Canada.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **insomnia**.
Support is limited: **no registered clinical trials** and **16 publications**, only one of which is a clinical efficacy trial in insomnia patients (a 1981 double-blind comparison with lormetazepam).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Diazepam is known to be a positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors. This produces sedative, anxiolytic and hypnotic effects, which makes a role in sleep disorders biologically plausible.

Benzodiazepines as a class are already used for short-term insomnia. The 1981 double-blind study (PMID 6113175) tested diazepam 5 mg directly in outpatients with sleep disorders. However, the other retrieved literature is general (benzodiazepine pharmacology reviews, GABA-A modulator reviews, herbal insomnia studies). The very high TxGNN score is a model prediction, not clinical evidence.

Long-term risks limit enthusiasm. These include dependence, disruption of sleep architecture, and a reported association between long-term benzodiazepine and Z-drug use and breast cancer progression.

## Safety Considerations

- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions are on record. Examples:
  - **Major**: morphine.
  - **Moderate**: bupropion, aprepitant, omeprazole, esomeprazole, cimetidine, clarithromycin, cisapride, clotrimazole, miconazole, dronabinol, metoclopramide.
  - **Minor**: dexamethasone, betamethasone and several antacids (magnesium oxide, aluminum hydroxide, calcium carbonate).
- **Literature-reported concerns**: dependence and withdrawal, cognitive decline, falls in older adults, and a possible association with breast cancer progression with long-term use.

Please refer to the package insert for full warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The evidence is limited to one small, older double-blind comparison trial in insomnia patients, which supports an L2 level. No placebo-controlled trials of diazepam for insomnia were found. Long-term safety concerns and the lack of label safety data weigh against proceeding.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from Health Canada
- Detailed mechanism of action data
- Placebo-controlled or modern comparative trial data for diazepam in insomnia
- A benefit-risk assessment for short-term use, with a dependence and falls mitigation plan for older adults
