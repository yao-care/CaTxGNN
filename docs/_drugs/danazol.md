# Danazol: From Endometriosis to Amenorrhea (Menstrual Suppression)

## One-Sentence Summary

Danazol is a synthetic steroid originally used to treat endometriosis and fibrocystic breast disease.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be useful for **amenorrhea** (induced menstrual suppression), with **no registered clinical trials** and **20 publications** (mostly reviews and older clinical studies) currently supporting this direction.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data are not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on the literature, danazol is a synthetic steroid derived from 17α-ethinyltestosterone. It suppresses the pituitary-ovarian axis, reducing the midcycle LH/FSH surge, and produces an anovulatory, low-oestrogen, androgenic state with endometrial atrophy.

This is the same effect that makes danazol work in endometriosis. Endometriotic lesions regress when the ovaries are down-regulated, as in amenorrhoea or menopause. Amenorrhea is therefore an expected result of treatment, and the link between the original and predicted indications is mechanistically coherent.

There is an important caveat. Amenorrhea is a therapeutic effect rather than a disease. The use here is menstrual suppression, not treatment of a pathology. A 2024 retrospective multi-site cohort study describes danazol being used for menstrual suppression in transgender and nonbinary individuals, where its androgenic effects can be an added concern.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications suggested by the knowledge graph** (these come from drug-disease relationship data, not from package insert text): epilepsy, porphyria (including inherited porphyria and porphyrin metabolism disorders), kidney disease, liver disease, gallbladder disease, female breast carcinoma, migraine, and familial combined hyperlipidaemia.
- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were identified, and 20 are available for review. Most are rated Moderate, and many involve antidiabetic agents such as acarbose, canagliflozin, glimepiride, glipizide, glyburide, insulin, and GLP-1 agonists. Cisapride is rated **Major**. Budesonide and eliglustat are also listed as Moderate.
- **Literature-reported interactions**: Two case-level reports describe a clinically significant increase in carbamazepine levels with danazol (almost twofold) and rhabdomyolysis with pancreatitis when danazol is combined with lovastatin.
- **Androgenic effects**: Reversible effects such as pigmentation of vellus hairs and voice changes are described in the menstrual suppression literature.
- Please refer to the package insert for the official warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The mechanistic link is coherent and the literature supports amenorrhea as a known effect of danazol. However, there are no clinical trials, the evidence is mostly old reviews and small studies, and amenorrhea is a treatment effect rather than a disease target. Official safety data are also missing, so the evidence is not enough to move forward.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Health Canada package insert warnings and contraindications (the Canadian licence records currently have no indication text)
- Mechanism of action data, for example from DrugBank
- A defined use case for menstrual suppression, such as endometriosis-related or in gender-affirming care, with prospective or controlled evidence
- A risk-benefit review of androgenic effects, the contraindications above, and the carbamazepine and statin interactions
- Comparison with established alternatives such as continuous oral contraceptives and GnRH analogues
