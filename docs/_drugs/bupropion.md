# Bupropion: From Depression and Nicotine Dependence to Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder

## One-Sentence Summary

Bupropion is an antidepressant and smoking-cessation aid, originally used for major depressive disorder and nicotine dependence.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)**,
with **6 clinical trials** (including 1 completed Phase 3 placebo-controlled trial) and **20 publications** currently supporting this direction.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on general pharmacological knowledge, bupropion inhibits the reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine (an NDRI) and has no serotonergic activity.

ADHD is linked to dysfunction of the noradrenergic and dopaminergic systems, and established ADHD medicines act on these same pathways. Bupropion's effect on these pathways offers a plausible way to improve attention and executive function. Its efficacy in depression is established, and depression, anxiety and nicotine dependence commonly occur together with ADHD. Smoking-cessation data in people with ADHD symptoms also add supporting context.

Published reviews describe bupropion as a second-line or non-stimulant option, typically for adults who do not respond to or cannot tolerate stimulants. It is not a first-line treatment.

## Safety Considerations

**Contraindications flagged in the knowledge graph** (derived from drug-disease relationships, not from label text):
- Neurological: epilepsy
- Psychiatric: manic bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders (including schizoaffective and schizophreniform disorders), anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, substance abuse/dependence, opiate dependence
- Cardiovascular: hypertension, myocardial infarction
- Liver, kidney and other: liver disease and failure, cirrhosis, kidney disease, gallbladder disease, central nervous system neoplasm, hemoglobinopathy

**Drug Interactions:** 100 interactions were identified. Examples rated Major include:
- Opioids: fentanyl, alfentanil, codeine, dihydrocodeine, tramadol
- Other: methylphenidate, lidocaine, hydrocortisone, aldesleukin, amantadine

Moderate interactions include alcohol, alprazolam, nifedipine, hydrochlorothiazide, cetirizine and others.

The methylphenidate interaction is relevant to ADHD, since stimulants are first-line therapy. Bupropion is also known to lower the seizure threshold. Official package insert warnings were not available and should be reviewed before any clinical use.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
A completed Phase 3 placebo-controlled trial, a Phase 2/3 trial, a Phase 4 trial and a Cochrane review directly address bupropion in ADHD, and bupropion is already marketed in Canada. Most trials are small and enrol patients with substance-use comorbidity, and bupropion is not a first-line ADHD treatment.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Health Canada package insert warnings and contraindications (a blocking gap for safety screening)
- Confirmation of the results and effect size of NCT00048360 and the Cochrane review
- Mechanism of action data from DrugBank
- A seizure-risk and interaction management plan, especially for patients on stimulants or opioids
- Review of whether comorbid substance-use populations generalize to the general ADHD population

**Other predictions:** ADHD, inattentive type has limited, mostly extrapolated support (L3, Research Question). The remaining predictions (faciodigitogenital syndrome, specific developmental disorder, chondromyxoid fibroma, 16p11.2 microdeletion syndrome, hypervitaminosis, benign paroxysmal torticollis of infancy, trigeminal nerve neoplasm, hypertelorism) have no supporting evidence and no plausible mechanism, and should be held.
