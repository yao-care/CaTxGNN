## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data from DrugBank is not available. Based on known pharmacology, prednisolone is a glucocorticoid that suppresses T-cell-mediated immune attack.

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune disease in which T cells attack the hair follicle, so an immunosuppressive corticosteroid is mechanistically plausible. Systemic and pulse corticosteroids are already used in practice for severe cases. One mechanistic study (PMID 30294905) suggests oral pulse steroids work partly by lowering TNF-α levels.

The main limits are relapse after withdrawal and cumulative steroid toxicity, which restrict long-term use. JAK inhibitors have also changed treatment in this field. Steroid pulses are now discussed mainly as an alternative when JAK inhibitors are unavailable or unsuitable (PMID 41243342).

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The mechanism is plausible and systemic steroids are already used in practice. However, the evidence is mostly small studies, reviews and trials of related steroids, with no Phase 3 trial of prednisolone in alopecia areata. Relapse after withdrawal and steroid toxicity limit its value, and JAK inhibitors are now a major competing option. The Canadian package insert safety data is also missing.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Health Canada package insert warnings and contraindications
- Mechanism of action data from DrugBank
- Full-text review of the 2005 placebo-controlled prednisolone study (PMID 15692475) to confirm its design and results
- A comparison with JAK inhibitors and a relapse-prevention and steroid-toxicity monitoring plan

Of the other 9 predictions, only idiopathic steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (L1) has strong support. That is confirmation of existing standard of care rather than true repurposing, and the remaining predictions have little or no supporting evidence.
